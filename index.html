Chapter 1

The Truth of the Center: A Traditional Imami Account

I. The Center

Many Shia today inherit a religious landscape filled with familiar words carrying unfamiliar meanings. Ahlul Bayt (as), Imam Hussain (as), wilayah, azadari, irfan, scholarship, justice, Mahdism the vocabulary remains sacred, yet its center is often displaced. This confusion has a history. Across centuries, various currents arose claiming to preserve religion while slowly relocating divine guidance away from its only protected locus: the Masoom Imam (as).

Imami Shiism begins with a severe and luminous claim: divine guidance is not left to history, consensus, charisma, institution, intellect, or spiritual self-claiming. It is preserved through ismah and wilayah eternal realities disclosed in creation through Muhammad (s) and the AhlulBayt (as), the Masoom Imams (as), the hujjaj of Allah upon every age. Hadith al-Thaqalayn gives this binding truth its temporal and metaphysical arc: from here to the Pond, the Qur'an and the Ahlul Bayt (as) remain joined. The believer is not handed a text and abandoned to interpretation; he is bound to a living divine guidance that crosses time, concealment, death, resurrection, and return.

Ismah protects divine guidance from corruption. Wilayah makes divine guidance present, accessible, and salvific. The earth, in the Imami corpus, is never empty of Allah's hujjah, whether apparent or hidden. The tradition that whoever dies without knowing the Imam of his age dies the death of jahiliyyah leaves no room for vague spirituality, improvised authority, or pious self-appointment. Recognition of the Imam is the grammar of salvation.

This is the brilliance of the Imami schema: it refuses to let religion collapse into the world's categories. Power, lineage, state, scholarship, spiritual charisma, and social consensus all fail before the protected guide. In Karbala, this truth becomes inexhaustible. Hussain (as) manifests wilayah so sovereignly that the world's order of things is rendered meaningless: armies, victory, defeat, rule, survival, calculation, and history itself stand exposed before divine reality.

II. The Original Imami Corpus

The earliest Shia traditionalist corpus grew under the shadow of the living Imams: especially from Imam al-Sadiq (as), Imam al-Kazim (as), Imam al-Rida (as), Imam al-Jawad (as), Imam al-Hadi (as), Imam al-Askari (as), and into the Minor Occultation of Imam al-Mahdi (aj). The community was formed through hadith, notebooks, letters, questions and answers, legal instruction, ziyarat, dua, mourning, and disciplined transmission.

Works such as al-Mahasin, Basair al-Darajat, al-Kafi, Kamil al-Ziyarat, Kitab al-Ghaybah, and the writings of al-Saduq carried forward a world formed around the Imam's word, the Imam's light, the Imam's grief, and the believer's attachment to wilayah.

The ethic was simple and profound. The scholar served the transmitted inheritance. He preserved, arranged, explained, and protected. He stood beneath the word of the Masoom Imam (as). His dignity came from fidelity, not self-authorizing originality. The traditionalist was a guardian of the original divine grammar: hujjah, ismah, wilayah, ma'rifah, ziyarat, shafa'ah, azadari, ghaybah, and sacred attachment to the AhlulBayt (as).

This protection did not end with the earliest compilers. Later traditionalists carried the trust across centuries. Ibn Tawus preserved the intimacy of dua, ziyarat, sacred time, and longing for the Hidden Imam (aj). Majlisi gathered the oceanic memory of the AhlulBayt (as) before it could be thinned by rationalist embarrassment. Hurr al-Amili anchored law in hadith without closing the sacred excess of devotion. Sayyid Hashim al-Bahrani kept the Qur'an bound to Imamic tafsir. Yusuf al-Bahrani gave learned resistance to the full triumph of juristic rationalism. Their greatness lay in protecting the inherited world, not replacing it with themselves.

This traditionalist stream protected Shiism from being flattened into theology, mysticism, law, or politics. Its task was more sacred: to preserve the conditions through which divine light remains accessible in the age of concealment.

III. Hussain (as) as the Illumination of Shiism

At the center of this divine grammar stands Imam Hussain (as).

Imami Shiism becomes most luminous through Hussain (as): through grief, remembrance, and sacred sovereign self-sacrifice. Karbala is the supreme disclosure of wilayah. Hussain (as) stands before the entire apparatus of worldly meaning and renders it weightless. Army, administration, coercion, courtly rhetoric, tribal calculation, political authority, and even the scribes of history are exposed as small and vacuous before the brilliance of divine reality. Around Hussain (as), the world's claim to seriousness dissolves: sovereignty, strategy, status, ideology, scholarship without ismah, spirituality without wilayah, and every attempt to make divine truth answerable to history.

Karbala also evacuates worldly epistemologies. It shatters the illusion that reality belongs to what human beings can govern, classify, domesticate, conquer, rationalize, or make useful. The deepest reality is where Allah places His light. The world looks for sovereignty in force, office, institution, argument, technique, and state. Shiism answers with grief: the tear becoming a portal to divine nearness.

This is why traditional Shiism produces beauty. It produces majlis, marsiya, noha, soz, alam, zarih, procession, hospitality, fragrance, craft, poetry, tears, and touch. These are forms generated by an ontology. Ordinary speech cannot bear Hussain (as), so poetry rises. Private feeling alone cannot contain Hussain (as), so public remembrance unfolds. Individual piety cannot claim Hussain (as), so communities of mourning are formed.

Azadari is the heart disciplined by grief. Ziyarat is the soul moving toward divine nearness through Hussain (as). Majlis is the cultivation of sacred memory in which one mourner's love touches another's sorrow. The world imagines grief as embarrassment to be erased quickly. In Shiism, grief becomes knowledge, mercy, refinement, and attachment.

This is the civilization the traditionalists protected.

IV. The Kalāmī Reduction

Over time, three recurrent distortions emerged inside and around Shiism: the Kalāmī Reduction, the 'Irfanī Self-Inflation, and the Political Hollowing.

The first was the Kalāmī Reduction. Its early force appears in figures such as Shaykh Mufid, Sharif al-Murtada, and Shaykh Tusi, who defended Shiism through reason, debate, systematic theology, and legal theory. Their learning is beside the point. The reduction lies in the relocation of authority.

The scholar-theologian increasingly becomes the rational organizer of the Imamic inheritance. The transmitted word of the Imam begins to pass through the court of speculative method. A world received through ismah is made answerable to a faculty that carries no ismah.

This is the beginning of shrinkage. Ziyarat becomes exaggeration. Azadari becomes custom. Shafa'ah becomes discomfort. Eschatology becomes abstraction. The cosmic station of Fatima (sa), Ali (as), Hussain (as), and the Qa'im (aj) becomes negotiable before rational management. Reason has honor beneath the Imam. It becomes corrosive when enthroned above transmission.

The modern world is a grotesque fruition of worshipping epistemic dazzle: technique is sanctity, classification is wisdom, system is guidance. The same pathology enters religion when rational method domesticates divine infinitude. A polished argument cannot bear wilayah. A clever method remains a worldly instrument until disciplined by the hujjah of Allah.

V. The Irfānī Self-Inflation

The second distortion was the 'Irfanī Self-Inflation. Its language is ascent, unveiling, annihilation, perfected soul, sacred station, and the inward ladder of realization. In Shia vocabulary this can sound elevated. Its danger is precisely its beauty.

This strain appears most clearly where Ibn Arabi-style metaphysics enters Shia language: the Perfect Man, unveiling, spiritual hierarchy, and the soul's ascent toward sacred station. Sayyid Haydar Amuli tries to reconcile this metaphysical grammar with Imami doctrine. Mulla Sadra deepens the philosophical- irfānī architecture. Later figures carry the same vocabulary into modern Shia intellectual life. In its most consequential modern form, Khomeini fuses 'irfan, juristic authority, and political sovereignty into one unstable religious personality.

The issue is the inflation of the self into imagined proximity with what belongs only to divine appointment. Wilayah begins to resemble an acquired station. The Masoom Imam (as) is reduced into a mystical archetype, a horizon of self-realization, a symbol of spiritual ascent.

This is spiritual narcissism in sacred garments. The believer receives grace through the Imam. He loves, obeys, mourns, visits, remembers, and awaits. He does not become coextensive with the Imam. The station of the hujjah is not the achievement of a refined soul. It is the divine protection of guidance itself.

VI. The Political Hollowing

The third distortion is the Political Hollowing. Its modern form recasts Imam Ali (as), Imam Hussain (as), and Imam Mahdi (aj) through liberation, revolution, resistance, sovereignty, statecraft, and historical mobilization. Karbala becomes political energy. Mahdism becomes a horizon for juristic rule. The faqih, movement, or state speaks with borrowed sacred authority.

Its genealogy is visible in stages. Juristic authority expands under Safavid arrangements. Naraqi gives a broader formulation of the authority of the jurist. Na'ini tries to reconcile Shiism with constitutional order. Shariati turns Hussain (as) and martyrdom into revolutionary ideology. Khomeini converts the whole arc into state doctrine: wilayat al-faqih, mass mobilization, revolutionary sovereignty, and Mahdism as political horizon.

Its softer modern form is equally hollow: Hussain (as) conscripted into NGO morality, social-justice theater, environmental slogans, lifestyle ethics, therapeutic self-improvement, and every fashionable cause seeking sacred decoration. The modern self, incapable of generating sanctity, drags Hussain (as) into its pathetic little narratives of narcissistic virtue.

This is among the most vulgar modern mutilations of tashayyu. The state has no share in Karbala. Its grammar of sovereignty, security, mobilization, and historical necessity belongs to the very order Hussain (as) emptied of meaning. To drag Karbala into statecraft is reduction dressed as piety.

No faqih, party, revolution, militia, bureaucracy, or state can inherit wilayah. The ghaybah of the Imam (aj) is not a vacancy for ambitious men. Karbala cannot become strategy. Azadari cannot become mobilization. Mahdism cannot become a political horizon. The blood of Hussain (as) is not raw material for ideology.

VII. Their Cynical Convergence

These distortions do not remain isolated. They acquire a cynical resonance across time. The Kalāmī Reduction makes the Imam's transmitted world answerable to scholarly arbitration. The Irfanī Self-Inflation makes sacred station appear inwardly acquirable. The Political Hollowing then gathers both errors into power: wilayah rationally managed, mystically imitated, and finally captured by the state.

By the time the modern political project arrives, the theft has already been made thinkable. The faqih inherits the confidence of method. The mystic supplies the aura of sacred interiority. The revolutionary translates Karbala into mobilization. Eternity is reduced into history. The hidden hujjah is treated as an absence to be administered. Hussain (as) is made to serve the very world he rendered meaningless.

VIII. The Repeating Pathology

These distortions are not serious rival ontologies. They are recurring pathologies of religious history: fallible humanity trying to occupy a station that only divine protection can bear.

Rabbinic legalism relocated divine command into a self-authorizing interpretive caste. Christianity relocated divine presence into Pauline theology, councils, priesthood, church hierarchy, and papal institution. Sunni Islam performed a catastrophic counterfeit of ismah by sacralizing Companions, consensus, caliphate, juristic schools, and inherited political outcomes. Sufi formations often relocated sacred authority into the master of unveiling and the self that claims arrival.

The costumes change. The pathology remains.

The rabbi, priest, caliph, companion, consensus, theologian, mystic, jurist, movement, and state each claim to guard religion. Each begins by mediating. Each eventually competes with the protected guide. Divine authority is interpreted, then managed, then appropriated, then replaced.

IX. The Return

Imami Shiism alone gives the coherent answer: guidance belongs to the Masoom Imam (as).

The scholar's reason cannot bear wilayah. The mystic's self cannot bear wilayah. The jurist, movement, and state cannot bear wilayah. Companions, consensus, caliphs, churches, councils, priesthoods, and interpretive castes cannot manufacture divine protection. They may organize, argue, administer, inspire, or preserve. They cannot become hujjah.

A coherent Shia return gives every human activity its proper rank. Learning has honor beneath the AhlulBayt (as). Spiritual refinement has beauty beneath the Ahlul Bayt (as). Public order, law, and worldly administration remain worldly necessities; they do not become sacred by borrowing the language of Karbala, wilayah, or Mahdism. They become corrupt the instant they pretend to share in ismah or wilayah. The center belongs only to the Masoom Imam (as).

The traditionalists protected this rank-order. Their greatness lies in submission to the original divine schema. They preserved the transmitted inheritance against every age's preferred arrogance: rationalist control, mystical self-expansion, legal caste authority, political capture, and institutional ambition.

Shiism remains coherent through return to the Masoom Imam (as): the hujjah of Allah, the light of wilayah, the majesty of Fatima (sa), the criterion of Ali (as), the grief of Hussain (as), and the living presence of the Hidden Imam (aj).

At its most luminous, Shiism is divine light made present through grief and remembrance. It is the path where Hussain (as) shatters the illusion of worldly power, where tears become knowledge, where mourning becomes mercy, where beauty rises from sacred sorrow, and where the believer remains attached to Allah through the AhlulBayt (as).

Everything else is religion after the theft of its center.

Chapter 2

The Revolutionary Capture of Karbala and Wilayah

A Traditional Imami Repudiation of “Resistance” and Wilāyat al-Faqīh

The religious ideology of the Islamic Republic depends upon two connected appropriations. Karbala is reconstructed as a permanent political program called “resistance”; the power to interpret and command that program is then vested in the ruling faqīh. The blood of Hussain (a.s.) gives the state its sacred vocabulary, while wilāyat al-faqīh supplies the hierarchy capable of directing it.

This structure required a profound alteration of the inherited Imami world. Karbala’s singularity had to become transferable. Grief had to acquire political utility. Azadari had to be judged by its capacity to mobilize. The ghaybah had to appear as a deficit of visible government. The faqīh had to receive the Imam’s public and coercive functions while formally disclaiming his metaphysical station.

The traditionalist corpus preserves a radically different order. Hussain’s day remains incomparable. Azadari possesses an intrinsic sacred reality. The vindication of Hussain remains attached to the divinely supported Imam from the family of Muhammad. Premature banners are treated with suspicion. Political turbulence does not disclose its own divine meaning. The ghaybah intensifies recognition of the living hujjah and vigilance against claimants. Imamate remains indivisible from divine appointment, protection from error, sacred knowledge and an incomparable station inaccessible to scholarly achievement or institutional selection.

The argument does not depend upon a single isolated narration. It emerges cumulatively from the architecture of the early corpus: al-Kulaynī’s al-Kāfī, al-Nuʿmānī’s Kitāb al-Ghaybah, Ibn Qulawayh’s Kāmil al-Ziyārāt, al-Ṣadūq’s al-Amālī and Kamāl al-Dīn, Ibn Ṭāwūs’s al-Luhūf, and al-Majlisī’s later preservation of the occultation traditions.

I. Karbala’s incomparable reality

The slogan “Every day is Ashura and every land is Karbala” has become the compact creed of revolutionary Shiism. Khomeini’s official archive presents the phrase as a mandate to treat Ashura as continuously present in later struggles. Its political function is transparent: Karbala becomes indefinitely reproducible, available for application to successive wars, movements, leaders and states. (Imam Khomeini Portal, “Every Day Is Ashura and Every Land Is Karbala.”)

Al-Ṣadūq preserves the opposite theological movement. Imam al-Hasan (a.s.) tells Hussain (a.s.):

لَا يَوْمَ كَيَوْمِكَ يَا أَبَا عَبْدِ اللهِ

“There is no day like your day, O Aba Abdillah.”

The report proceeds through the shedding of Hussain’s blood, the desecration of his sanctity, the captivity of his family and the mourning of creation. Karbala is established as an event without analog. (Al-Ṣadūq, al-Amālī, majlis 24, report 3.)

Ibn Ṭāwūs carries the same declaration into al-Luhūf, embedding it within a work whose entire form is lamentation before a sacred catastrophe rather than the extraction of a portable political method. (Ibn Ṭāwūs, al-Luhūf.)

The difference between the two formulations is decisive. “No day resembles your day” gathers every later grief before Hussain (a.s.). “Every day is Ashura” distributes Hussain across the conflicts of history. The first preserves the immeasurable distance between Karbala and ordinary political struggle. The second removes that distance so that political struggle may inherit Karbala’s sacred intensity.

Once Karbala becomes transferable, every state-approved battlefield may acquire its aura. Political opponents become Yazids; allied combatants become Hussaini martyrs; military endurance becomes Ashura; geopolitical policy becomes fidelity to the blood of Hussain. The singular calamity is reduced into an ideological technology of permanent mobilization.

II. The world preserved in Kāmil al-Ziyārāt

Ibn Qulawayh’s Kāmil al-Ziyārāt reveals how the early traditionalist world actually received Hussain (a.s.). Its chapters gather the mourning of the heavens and earth, the lamentation of angels and jinn, the tears of Imam Zayn al-Abidin, poetry and weeping, remembrance while drinking water, pilgrimage undertaken through love and yearning, intercession, the sanctity of Karbala, the soil of the grave and the nearness attained through ziyarat. (Ibn Qulawayh, Kāmil al-Ziyārāt, especially the chapters on mourning, visitation and the sanctity of Karbala.)

This is neither an ornamental margin around a political message nor an emotional preparation for a revolutionary program. It is a complete sacred world. The believer encounters Hussain through grief, visitation, attachment, tears, poetry, hospitality, remembrance, curse upon his enemies, longing and hope for intercession.

The corpus does not apologize for the salvific power of grief. Those who cry, cause others to cry, recite poetry, visit Hussain or yearn for his grave receive immense spiritual rewards. The visitor of Hussain is described through proximity to the Prophet, Ali and Fatima; ziyarat erases sins, relieves grief and opens intercession. (Ibn Qulawayh, Kāmil al-Ziyārāt.)

The political reconstruction of Karbala therefore required an assault upon this devotional plenitude. Tears had to demonstrate social usefulness. Majlis had to awaken a political consciousness. Mourning had to produce resistance. Ziyarat had to yield an ethical program legible to revolutionary intellectuals. Grief absorbed in Hussain himself became vulnerable to accusations of passivity, superstition, distortion or ritual excess.

The traditionalist corpus recognizes no such deficiency. Grief is already a mode of knowledge. Love is already an approach to Allah. Ziyarat is already movement toward the Imam. Tears are already purification, attachment and mercy. The majlis requires no political outcome to justify its existence.

Azadari arises from the resonance of divine reality in the grief and love of Hussain (a.s.). Its validity does not depend upon service to history.

III. The vengeance of Hussain remains with the Imam

Ziyarat Ashura contains the most intense language of allegiance and dissociation. The believer approaches Allah through love of Hussain, curses those who founded oppression against the AhlulBayt, declares peace with those at peace with Hussain and enmity toward those at war with him.

Yet the ziyarat does not place the armed vindication of Hussain in the hands of every movement that claims resistance. The supplicant asks Allah to sustain him in seeking Hussain’s vengeance with the exclusively supported Imam of AhlulBayt , and again with name Mahdi who will speak for Hussain. (Ibn Qulawayh, Kāmil al-Ziyārāt, chap. 71.)

The wording preserves a crucial distinction. Grief is immediate. Loyalty is immediate. Barāʾah is immediate. The yearning for vindication is immediate. The divinely authorized fulfillment remains attached to the Imam.

Revolutionary theology removes this reservation without openly denying it. The state identifies the contemporary battlefield, names the contemporary Yazid, determines the legitimate front, confers martyrdom upon its dead and presents its armed policy as the living continuation of Ashura.

The sacred desire preserved beneath the Imam’s authority becomes political capital administered by the state.

The traditional ziyarat allows no such transfer. Its fire is real, but its destination is the supported Imam. The longing of the believer cannot be converted into a general license for revolutionary organizations, national armies or juristic governments.

IV. The banner before the Qāʾim

Al-Kulaynī transmits from Imam al-Sadiq (a.s.):

كُلُّ رَايَةٍ تُرْفَعُ قَبْلَ قِيَامِ الْقَائِمِ فَصَاحِبُهَا طَاغُوتٌ

“Every banner raised before the rising of the Qāʾim—its bearer is a ṭāghūt.” (Al-Kulaynī, al-Kāfī, Rawḍat al-Kāfī, report 452.)

The report should be read with precision. It need not turn every act of personal defense, communal protection or opposition to injustice into a sin. Its object is the banner: an organized center of command, obedience, allegiance and sacrifice.

The banner becomes spiritually dangerous when it absorbs the sacred allegiance belonging to the hujjah. Its leader acquires authority over life and death. Its political enemies become enemies of the divine cause. Its casualties become redemptive martyrs. Loyalty to its strategy becomes a measure of religious fidelity.

This danger is built into the Iranian revolutionary system. “Resistance” is no longer a contingent political or military response. It becomes an identity, a school, a sacred front and an enduring interpretation of Karbala. The state’s political map is overlaid with Ashura’s moral absolutes.

The language of resistance then performs the work of divine appointment without formally claiming Imamate. The banner summons obedience through the names of Hussain, Zaynab, Mahdi and martyrdom. A worldly leadership acquires a sacred claim over the believer’s conscience.

Al-Kulaynī’s report names precisely this mutation: the banner becomes a rival destination of devotion.

V. Al-Nuʿmānī and the discipline of waiting

Al-Nuʿmānī’s Kitāb al-Ghaybah is among the earliest sustained attempts to form the Shiʿa religiously for the Major Occultation. Completed in 342 AH, close to the beginning of the ghaybah al-kubrā, it emerged from the transmission world of al-Kulaynī, from whom al-Nuʿmānī received a substantial part of his learning. Its arrangement is itself an argument. The book proceeds through the permanent necessity of the hujjah, divine designation of the Twelve, the exposure of pretenders, patience during concealment, the confusion that overtakes the community, the prohibition of fixing dates and the signs by which the true affair may finally be recognized. It offers no institutional program for replacing the absent manifestation of the Imam. It teaches the community how to remain within his authority when every visible circumstance tempts it to seek another center. (Al-Nuʿmānī, Kitāb al-Ghaybah, introduction and chaps. 4–14.)

The reports collected in the chapter on patience give this formation a severe political content. When black banners appeared in Khurasan, Imam al-Sadiq (a.s.) was asked how the Shiʿa should respond. He instructed them to remain where they were until the AhlulBayt were visibly united around a man, and only then to hasten with their weapons. Other reports in the same chapter advise believers to restrain their tongues, remain in their homes and resist movements whose religious claims cannot be authenticated by the appointed Imam. (Al-Nuʿmānī, Kitāb al-Ghaybah, chap. 11, especially reports 1–8.)

The geography and imagery are unusually significant. Khurasan, revolutionary banners, an oppressive order under pressure and claims of impending justice provide exactly the kind of historical drama from which later movements would infer providential authorization. Al-Nuʿmānī’s corpus refuses that inference. Neither the righteousness of a declared objective nor the intensity of sacrifice enables a movement to locate itself within the Imam’s sacred mandate. The criterion remains the manifest agreement of the AhlulBayt around the divinely appointed man.

The repeated warning that “the hasteners perish” gives ghaybah a theological depth often lost in political readings of expectation. Delay is not an administrative defect in the divine order. It is the form of the ordeal. The believer must endure the absence of public manifestation without filling it through urgency, charisma or institutional ingenuity. In al-Nuʿmānī, the multiplication of banners is not evidence that the promised order is gradually being assembled; it is one of the conditions under which discernment becomes most difficult and fidelity most costly.

This is why al-Nuʿmānī’s work is more than a collection of predictions. It describes a religious anthropology of occultation. Hearts turn, communities divide, impatient believers become vulnerable to claimants, and political events invite eschatological overinterpretation. The correct response is formed through knowledge of the Imam, submission to Allah’s timing and suspicion toward every movement that converts longing into obedience to itself. Al-Majlisī later reproduces this discipline within the occultation sections of Biḥār al-Anwār, preserving patience, piety and restraint as central virtues of the Shiʿa during concealment. (Al-Majlisī, Biḥār al-Anwār, vol. 52.)

Iranian revolutionary theology reverses the spiritual grammar of these reports. Historical impatience becomes an achievement. The ground must be prepared, sovereignty made visible and the awaited order anticipated through a juristic state. Ghaybah ceases to be a divinely sustained condition in which the living hujjah remains the sole sacred center; it becomes a political interval to be managed by men who claim to realize the Imam’s governmental purpose in advance of him.

VI. Recognition of the Imam is sufficient during delay

Al-Kulaynī devotes an entire chapter to the teaching that the early or late appearance of the Qāʾim does not harm the believer who knows his Imam. Imam al-Sadiq tells Zurārah: “Know your Imam; once you know him, it will not harm you whether this affair comes earlier or later.” Other reports in the chapter place the believer who dies in this recognition among those standing beneath the Qāʾim’s banner. (Al-Kulaynī, al-Kāfī, Kitāb al-Ḥujjah, chap. 84.)

This teaching closes the psychological opening through which revolutionary substitution enters.

The Shiʿa do not require a visible political replica in order to belong to the cause of the Qāʾim. They do not need an interim supreme leader, a preliminary Imamic state or a surrogate banner to overcome the apparent emptiness of occultation. Recognition and fidelity already establish their relation to the Imam.

The modern political project cannot tolerate this sufficiency. It seeks visible sovereignty, command, territory, institutions, mobilization and historical agency. The hidden banner is anticipated through a state banner; the awaited authority is made present through juristic government.

The corpus locates participation in maʿrifah. The revolutionary state relocates it in political allegiance.

VII. Confusing banners and the danger of imposture

Al-Kulaynī preserves the warning that during the occultation multiple confusing banners will arise while the true affair of the AhlulBayt remains clearer than the sun. Al-Nuʿmānī places parallel reports within a larger account of confusion, testing and the difficulty of recognizing truth during concealment. The believer confronted by a claimant is directed toward the knowledge proper to the true possessor of the affair rather than toward charisma, political success or public acclamation. (Al-Kulaynī, al-Kāfī, Kitāb al-Ḥujjah, chap. 80; al-Nuʿmānī, Kitāb al-Ghaybah, chap. 10.)

Al-Nuʿmānī devotes a separate chapter to pretenders to Imamate and follows it with chapters on the Twelve appointed by Allah and the earth’s permanent need for the hujjah. The sequence is theological: counterfeit authority is intelligible only against the prior reality of divine designation. (Al-Nuʿmānī, Kitāb al-Ghaybah, chaps. 5–8.)

The final communication preserved by al-Ṣadūq to Ali ibn Muhammad al-Samarrī makes the boundary still more severe. The fourth special deputy is instructed to appoint no successor because the Major Occultation has begun. The communication then warns that claimants will arise during the extended concealment. (Al-Ṣadūq, Kamāl al-Dīn wa Tamām al-Niʿma, chap. 45, report 46.)

The Major Occultation thus opens through the termination of the publicly designated chain of special deputies. The text supplies no new public office inheriting that succession. It announces prolonged concealment, hardening hearts, confusion and false claims.

This matters enormously for the later theory of general deputyship. During the Lesser Occultation, representation was personal, named and explicitly established. Its continuation is then prohibited. Later juristic constructions transform a terminated, specific and verifiable relation into a general and permanent status attached to scholarly qualification.

The scale of the claim grows as direct verification disappears.

The mujtahid comes to speak bindingly in the Imam’s law. The marjaʿ becomes the destination of obligatory imitation. The juristic class administers religious wealth and communal affairs. The ruling faqīh finally claims the Imam’s governmental functions and public obedience.

The traditional corpus treats occultation as the supreme age of vigilance against counterfeit centers. The later edifice converts mediation into an institution.

VIII. Imamate as an indivisible divine office

The most damaging traditionalist text for wilāyat al-faqīh is al-Kulaynī’s extended report from Imam al-Riḍā (a.s.) on the nature of Imamate.

The Imam is described as Allah’s trustee over creation, His hujjah over His servants and His representative in the lands. He preserves religion and maintains its order. He is free from sin and fault, possesses special knowledge and is incomparable to any scholar. No one can approach his rank; no substitute can perform what he performs; his virtues are bestowed by Allah rather than acquired. Human intellects are incapable of selecting him. (Al-Kulaynī, al-Kāfī, Kitāb al-Ḥujjah, chap. 15, report 1.)

The report joins what the later political theory separates.

Religious order, judgment, public protection, authority and obedience arise from the same divine appointment that gives the Imam his knowledge, purity and incomparable station. Government does not appear as a detachable collection of functions that may be removed from ismah and transferred to a scholar.

Khomeini’s doctrine depends precisely upon that detachment. He argues that during the occultation the just faqīh possesses the same governmental authority held by the Prophet and the Imams in mobilizing armies, appointing officials, collecting taxes, administering penalties and implementing law. He explicitly distinguishes spiritual status from governmental function. (Khomeini, Islamic Government, “The Form of Islamic Government.”)

The distinction is often presented as restraint, since the faqīh does not formally claim ismah or the Imam’s ontological station. Its political operation is considerably more radical. The sacred constitution of Imamate is divided: divine light, infallibility and incomparable knowledge remain with the hidden Imam, while command of the community, coercive law, taxation, punishment, war and public obedience pass to the jurist.

The cynicism lies in the distribution of relevance. The Imam’s sacred light is exalted in the sphere where it cannot constrain the state and declared unnecessary in the sphere where power is exercised. It remains available as transcendental prestige, devotional emotion and inherited legitimacy; the attributes regarded as historically effective are assigned to the faqīh. The metaphysical Imam is preserved above the political order while the indivisibility of his authority is denied below it. Sacred light becomes politically valuable as symbolism and politically inconvenient as a criterion of rule.

The traditional report does not permit that surgery. The Imam’s function flows from who the Imam is. His authority belongs to the divine constitution of Imamate, not to technical competence in jurisprudence.

IX. The constitutional transfer

The Constitution of the Islamic Republic gives institutional form to Khomeini’s theory. Article 5 states that during the occultation the “wilayah and leadership of the Ummah” devolve upon the qualified faqīh. Article 57 places the legislative, executive and judicial branches under the supervision of the “absolute wilāyat-e-amr.” Article 110 assigns the Leader direction of general policy, supreme command of the armed forces, declarations of war and peace, military mobilization, senior military and judicial appointments, confirmation of the presidency and specified powers of dismissal. (Constitution of the Islamic Republic of Iran, arts. 5, 57 and 110.)

The constitutional language of devolution exposes the governing conception of ghaybah. Concealment is treated as the circumstance under which the Imam’s public leadership becomes institutionally available to another office. The living hujjah remains affirmed in doctrine, while the political consequences of his living authority are neutralized.

The traditional corpus understands concealment as the continued authority of an Imam who is hidden from public manifestation. His wilayah does not lapse, his Imamate does not enter suspension and his absence does not create a jurisdiction awaiting reassignment. Al-Nuʿmānī’s entire discipline of waiting rests upon this continued center: visible alternatives proliferate precisely because the true authority remains concealed rather than vacated.

The Iranian arrangement preserves the Hidden Imam as a transcendent source of legitimacy while transferring operative command to the faqīh. Its post-revolutionary ceremonial grammar deepens the manipulation. Whether expressed through an empty chair, an unoccupied place, a pulpit invoking the absent Imam or the recurrent claim that government merely prepares for his return, the symbolism appears to honor the reserve of ghaybah while depriving that reserve of constitutional consequence. The place remains ceremonially unoccupied after the functions of authority have already been exercised elsewhere. The Imam is made intensely present as symbol and systematically absent as a limit upon power. The state requires his concealment to leave political room for the faqīh and his sacred presence to consecrate the authority exercised in that room.

X. Resistance and juristic sovereignty form one system

Resistance theology and juristic sovereignty form a mutually reinforcing system. Karbala supplies a vocabulary of absolute moral conflict, while the faqīh and the institutions subordinate to him determine where that vocabulary will be applied. The state identifies the contemporary field of resistance, names legitimate allies and enemies, directs military and diplomatic policy and confers the status of martyrdom upon deaths occurring within that structure.

Muharram then provides the devotional environment through which those political determinations enter sacred memory. Contemporary casualties are placed within the imagery of Karbala, present enemies are interpreted through the Umayyad paradigm and strategic fidelity to the state is invested with the emotional force of fidelity to Hussain. The faqīh authorizes the political struggle, and the sacralized struggle in turn confirms the religious importance of the faqīh’s leadership.

This circularity enables contingent decisions of statecraft to acquire a status exceeding ordinary moral and political judgment. Defense, alliance, regional influence and military intervention may each be evaluated on their own circumstances. Resistance theology instead places them within an inherited eschatological drama whose principal identities have already been morally settled. The effect is to shelter policy within Karbala’s absolutes and to convert disagreement over strategy into suspicion of religious disloyalty.

The Iranian state thereby draws upon Hussain, Zaynab, martyrdom and the Qāʾim as sources of sacred intelligibility while retaining institutional control over their present application. Karbala becomes the grammar through which power narrates itself; wilāyat al-faqīh determines the authorized meaning of that grammar.

XI. The simultaneous degradation and occupation of azadari

The revolutionary appropriation of Karbala has always involved an ambivalent relationship with traditional azadari. Its intellectual reformers scrutinized popular mourning through the categories of authenticity, social usefulness and political awakening. Mutahhari’s intervention extended beyond the correction of unreliable narratives: he rejected the understanding of Hussain’s action as a unique command and insisted upon its general ideological and emulative productivity. Shariati translated the same impatience into a more explicit revolutionary vocabulary, contrasting a religion of martyrdom with a religion of mourning and placing Hussain within a continuous historical struggle. (Mutahhari, “‘Ashura—Misrepresentations and Distortions,” third sermon; Shariati, “Red Shiʿism—Black Shiʿism” and Martyrdom.)

The inherited corpus moves through another register. Ibn Qulawayh’s chapters on grief, lamentation, visitation, yearning, intercession and sacred proximity do not treat the majlis as an incomplete political assembly or tears as dormant revolutionary energy. Their religious force arises from the reality of Hussain and the believer’s attachment to him.

The revolutionary state nevertheless remains deeply dependent upon the devotional world its intellectual formation has sought to correct. Political meetings cannot reproduce the sacred intensity accumulated within Muharram. The state therefore enters the majlis with contemporary leaders, state martyrs, approved wars, geopolitical enemies and revolutionary instruction. Inherited grief is retained because of its power and disciplined because of its autonomy.

This dependence reveals the severity of the appropriation. Mourning absorbed in Hussain may be dismissed as ritualism or political unconsciousness, while mourning redirected toward the state’s dead is celebrated as living Ashura. Popular forms carried by rural, working-class and ordinary Shiʿa are subjected to the judgment of an educated ideological class shaped by sociology, revolutionary theory and modern ideas of historical agency. The mourner’s knowledge is recognized only after it has been translated into the categories of political usefulness.

Traditional azadari did not require such accreditation. It received the learned and unlearned through grief, love, vow, poetry, hospitality and visitation. Its forms could be assessed from within the inherited disciplines of religion without submitting their meaning to the program of a state. The revolutionary project has therefore neither simply abolished nor faithfully preserved azadari. It has attempted to occupy its devotional authority while redirecting its public meaning.

Conclusion

The Iranian revolutionary synthesis represents a fundamental corruption of Imami religious authority. Its treatment of Karbala reduces an incomparable divine disclosure to a reusable political vocabulary. Hussain (a.s.) is drawn into the temporal calculations of the state, assigned to contemporary fronts, attached to military policy and made to furnish moral absolutes for conflicts whose purposes remain contingent, strategic and worldly. This is already a grave theological deformation. Hussain (a.s.) does not belong to history as one heroic figure among others, available for analogy and repetition. History stands exposed before him. Karbala judges history; it cannot be conscripted into it.

The same deformation governs the treatment of ghaybah. Al-Nuʿmānī preserves occultation as an ordeal of fidelity in which visible banners proliferate, impatience becomes spiritually dangerous and the believer remains bound to the living hujjah without manufacturing a substitute for him. The Iranian state converts this ordeal into an opportunity for institutional succession. Concealment becomes the condition under which the Imam’s public authority may be anticipated, administered and transferred to the faqīh. The living Imam is retained as transcendent legitimacy while his operative authority is absorbed by a fallible ruler selected through a human institution.

This arrangement approaches a quasi-heretical inversion of Imami theology. The Imam remains verbally supreme while the state confiscates the authority through which supremacy has meaning in the world. His light is celebrated where it cannot restrain power; his absence is invoked where it permits power to expand. The faqīh disclaims ismah, sacred knowledge and divine appointment, then claims command over law, taxation, punishment, war, armies and public obedience. The humility is formal. The appropriation is total.

The same cynicism governs the state’s relationship with azadari. Traditional mourning is subjected to suspicion whenever it remains absorbed in Hussain (a.s.), yet its emotional force is indispensable to the revolutionary order. The state enters the majlis, occupies its imagery, redirects its grief and places its own dead within Karbala’s field of sanctity. A devotional world generated by love, ziyarat, intercession and mourning is converted into a mechanism for political allegiance. The appropriation is severe precisely because the state cannot generate this sacred power from itself.

The traditionalist corpus leaves no warrant for this construction. Al-Ṣadūq and Ibn Ṭāwūs preserve the incomparability of Hussain’s day. Ibn Qulawayh preserves grief, ziyarat and intercession as realities complete in themselves. Al-Kulaynī and al-Nuʿmānī warn against premature banners, counterfeit centres and the spiritual peril of haste. Al-Kulaynī binds authority to an Imam whose knowledge, purity and appointment lie beyond human acquisition. Al-Ṣadūq records the termination of the special deputyship at the beginning of the Major Occultation.

Taken together, these sources expose the Iranian project for what it is: a state theology that appropriates Karbala, neutralizes ghaybah, fragments Imamate and converts inherited devotion into political capital. Its vocabulary remains Shia while its structure relocates sacred authority from the divinely appointed Imam to the jurist-state. The result is neither a continuation of traditional Imamism nor a legitimate accommodation to political necessity. It is a profound usurpation conducted in the language of fidelity.

Chapter 3

Hussain and Allah

I. The Span of Mercy

I am somewhat invested in poetry—not just as an art form or aesthetic, but as a phenomenal navigator of life—I was sitting here trying to think of a couplet that encapsulates this entire epic for Maula Hussain. There are countless ones available. But capturing the span of time from the 3rd of Shaban, 4th Hijri to the 10th of Muharram, 61st Hijri, there is a couplet by a contemporary poet. And perhaps it also relates to what I want to say.

"Whether it is Fitras in the heavens..." Fitras, for those who don't know, was a punished, condemned angel. For an unknown length of time, he was condemned to some vacant, desolate galaxy and planet. It's a long story of what happened, but Allah had punished him by taking away his magnificent wings, his ability to fly, and his agency to travel across the entire world and universe. He lay on that desolate planet, constantly reciting words of repentance. So this refers to that Fitras. His salvation basically took less than an amount of time that we can measure. On the 3rd of Shaban, 4th Hijri, when his companions were descending in enormous numbers to visit Imam Hussain, they brought him along. He was merely touched to the cradle, the crib of Imam Hussain, and all his faculties and more were restored. All his wings were restored and he still exists today; it is written in many books that he is at the forefront of the angels who weep for Imam Hussain and welcome his pilgrims.

So, the couplet begins: "Whether it is Fitras in the heavens..." So that happened on the 3rd of Shaban, 4th Hijri. "...or Hurr on the earth." Fitras is condemned, suffering Allah's punishment, and Hurr is right there, about to be among the most condemned creatures possible. You don't necessarily have to be Shia to assume that those who were involved in the war against Imam Hussain were damned, condemned, and removed from God's mercy. Just as Fitras was in the heavens, Hurr was on earth.

"Whether it is Fitras in the heavens, or Hurr on the earth,
The beggars are changing, but the door remains the same."

Right, so from the same door, two condemned entities—one angel, one human—received their salvation at the beginning of Imam Hussain's presence on this earth and right at the conclusion of it. Such is the span of Imam Hussain's mercy and grace.

II. The Preservation of Religion

I apologize, I will mix a bit of Urdu and English in my talk. The title of this is very simple: Hussain and Allah.

There is a sentence that is repeated countless times in Shia gatherings, and even in everyday conversations among common Muslims. However, the interpretation of that sentence is, in my opinion, very limited, actually impoverished, and maybe entirely wrong. That sentence is: "Imam Hussain saved Monotheism (Tawheed), saved the religion (Deen)."

Right, and we have no idea how. The evidence following that isn't particularly compelling. A very large number of humans in this world do not believe in Monotheism, nor do they believe in religion. Even a very large number of Muslims have religious beliefs that are not aligned with the beliefs of the Prophet's household or the Sultan of Karbala, Imam Hussain. So what exactly did he save? That's a claim, but it is substantiated by very weak evidence that Yazid was going to do something nefarious. There were people who, from a religious point of view, did and continue to do far worse things than what Yazid was capable of. It is naive to imagine that the entire confusion of human beings was removed when Yazid was humiliated. That's simply not true, empirically not true. It has no connection to reality or our actual experience.

III. The Quranic Dahriya

But this is a 100 percent fact, and it is such a profound fact that it applies to no one else but Imam Hussain. There are a couple of verses from the Quran, which you can look up yourselves; I am not a good reciter. There is a very interesting title for the 45th Surah of the Quran: Surah Al-Jathiyah. Its literal meaning is "to bring to the knees" (kneeling). When Allah will cause all nations to be kneeled—meaning all our arrogance will vanish at that time. In it, Allah mentions a specific type of people. This is a question, especially for the students: what's the word for atheists in Urdu? Dahriya.

Since when have these "Dahriya" (atheists) existed, and what is the meaning of "Dahriya"? When did the common understanding of Dahriyas emerge in the world? What's the origin of them—people who have a complete denial of God? What's their genealogy? Commonly, to make it easier, people attribute it to some sort of 18th or 19th-century European thought. Generally, communists, Karl Marx, and some other German sociologists are attributed as the original founders of Dahriyat (atheism). Obviously, that's wrong. "Dahr" literally means time.

This is the 24th verse of Surah Al-Jathiyah, which says: "And they say, 'There is not but our worldly life; we die and live, and nothing destroys us except time (dahr).' And they have of that no knowledge; they are only assuming." They say there is nothing except this world, and time will finish us. It has nothing to do with God; time will finish us. 'Dahr' (time) will destroy us. Allah calls them mere speculators. This term "Yazunnoon" (they speculate/assume) is used in the Quran.

So that's one speculation about God. Another speculation is described using this same term in Surah Al-Baqarah, the 78th verse: "And among them are unlettered ones who do not know the Scripture except in wishful thinking, but they are only assuming." They fabricate meanings of the book from their own minds. They are speculators making conjectures. And about them, the 79th verse says, "So woe to those who write the scripture..."—the ones who write a little and confuse people, exchanging something pathetic in return for the mercy of God.

The final verse is the 154th verse in Surah Ali 'Imran. When the Muslims fled during the Battle of Uhud, their assumption (zann) is mentioned, that they fabricated an imaginary thing about Allah: "assuming about Allah other than the truth - the assumption of ignorance." They speculated and fabricated the idea that if they had made the decision themselves regarding the battle of Uhud, they wouldn't have been slaughtered the way they just were. That is the crux of it in Surah Ali 'Imran.

IV. Hostage to Consciousness

So we have three types of people before us. One are the people who say that time is God; God is not God, time is God. The second say that Allah can be invented from our own experience. And the third say that if such a huge calamity falls upon us, it would have been better for us to think for ourselves instead of believing in some imaginary Allah. Right, so these are the three big issues with us humans.

We are very, very catastrophically held hostage to our consciousness, our mind—which we call intelligence—when it comes to God. Because the entire idea of God can be argued; it can be said that He is our own fabrication. Humans can very easily invent a God. A God who is bound by our expectations. A God based on our life experiences, whom we fear, we worry about provisions (Rizq), etc. We invent God associated with these things. And that's the fundamental argument of atheists and Dahriyas: that God is a projection of our own consciousness, and they have a reasonable case. Almost all gods that tend to exist in the world can be explained by this interpretation.

V. The Portal of Grief

This means that for Allah to remain Allah, three things are required which are not possible in the human condition.

Situation number one: Time. There is no world or planet more time-bound and unfortunate than ours. Everything—just now Kumail Sahab also checked the time—we are absolutely submerged by time. Every experience that we have, everything that exists is under the majesty (jabroot), under the force of time. Whatever exists will end in time; it's that simple, whether it is living or non-living.

And the impact of time is so immense that it keeps us completely oblivious to our reality, especially our connections. How many of you know the name of your great-great-grandfather? There is one person. If you go back six or seven generations, you wouldn't know. More so, how many of you remember your great-great-grandfather? We don't. We're not supposed to; we are creatures of time. If we are creatures of time, then our concept of God also exists within time. Whatever happened in our life, that becomes the definition of God.

In comes Imam Hussain.

One thing that is impossible to preserve among humans is grief. It is impossible. We can preserve some nonsense in the name of a festival, but what we cannot preserve is grief. This does not exist in our condition. You are here because of your great-great-grandfather. It is possible your great-great-grandfather was hanged by the British—my great-great-grandfather was, that's why I'm saying this—in a very heinous way. Do you care? You don't care. You don't even know the guy's name. That's the reality of being human.

So how can we associate and perceive a God that is not hostage to time? The only portal possible, the only aperture possible, the only lens possible, is Imam Hussain.

Now, there are observant Shias here, and casual Shias too. But no one can deny that there are millions of Shias who are completely devastated by grief. They get close to death out of their love for Imam Hussain. If you think this is happening because of some system, then by definition that system must exist somewhere else. If humans are capable of doing this, then let them do it somewhere else too.

A friend of mine, an anthropologist, passed away. I personally promised her a very substantial grant to find some small tribe anywhere in the world, even in Papua New Guinea, that remembers grief. What tribe in the world, what family, what region is there where some massive catastrophe hasn't occurred? It absolutely has. Countless troubles have befallen this city where we are sitting. Do you remember? How many of you are mourning the Aligarh Colony massacre in 1986? How many people were killed in the Aligarh Colony massacre? It happened.
